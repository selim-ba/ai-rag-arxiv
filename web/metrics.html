What the numbers mean

Every figure on the front page: what it measures, what it does not, and why it is worth reporting.

How the system is tested at all

A hand-written set of 40 questions about the 49 papers: 20 factual, 10 comparisons, 4 definitional, and 6 that the papers deliberately cannot answer. For the 34 answerable ones, the chunks that genuinely contain the answer were labelled by hand. Everything below is measured against that set.

Finding the right chunk

hit@5 — 0.676

Of the questions with a known answer, how often at least one correct chunk appears in the five the system retrieves.

This is the ceiling on everything else: the answer is written only from those five chunks, so if none of them contains the fact, no amount of language skill can recover it. Two-thirds is the honest state of this system.

hit@1 — 0.353

How often the very first chunk is a correct one.

Matters less than it looks here, because the answer is written from all five chunks, not just the first. It is useful for comparing ways of ranking — search by meaning, search by exact words, the two merged, the two merged with weights, and two kinds of re-ranking that reorder the shortlist — but it does not predict whether the answer will be right.

recall@5 — 0.578

Some questions need several facts. This is the share of the needed facts that made it into the five chunks, not just whether one did.

A question can score a “hit” and still be missing half of what it needs — this is the number that notices.

Quality of the answer

correctness — 0.375

Does the answer actually say what a reference answer, written by hand from the papers, says? Judged by a second model, checked against blind human labels.

The judge was checked against a human labelling the same answers without seeing its verdicts, and the two agree at Cohen’s κ +0.80. Cohen’s kappa measures how much two judges agree beyond what they would hit by chance alone: 0 is chance, 1 is perfect, and anything above about 0.8 is usually called strong. It is why this number, rather than the others, carries the quality claims. The value is low because retrieval is the bottleneck: an answer cannot be right about a chunk that was never retrieved.

faithfulness — not validated

Whether every claim in the answer is actually supported by the retrieved chunks.

Reported as an auditable signal rather than a measurement: two runs with nothing changed disagreed enough to flip the sign of its agreement statistic. A number that unstable cannot be used to decide anything.

Knowing when to say no

refusal accuracy — 0.925

Counts all four outcomes: answered when it should, refused when it should, and both mistakes. Six of the 40 questions are unanswerable on purpose.

Deliberately not “refusal rate”. A system that refuses everything scores a perfect refusal rate and is useless, so the measure has to count both kinds of mistake at once.

false refusal rate — 0.059

How often it declines a question it could actually have answered.

The price of caution, kept visible so the caution can be priced.

Understanding the question

routing accuracy — 1.000 on held-out questions

Given a question, does the system correctly decide whether it is about the papers in general, about specific named papers, or about the library itself?

Scored against a specification written before the router existed. The first version scored well while picking the wrong paper every time, because the corpus titles do not contain the names anyone uses — “V-JEPA” is filed as Revisiting Feature Prediction for Learning Visual Representations from Video. A hand-written table of aliases fixed it.

follow-up resolution — 13 of 18

“and does it train a policy network?” has to become “does PlaNet train a policy network?” before anything searches.

Measured on 18 written-out cases: a first question, a follow-up, and what the follow-up should be understood to mean. It gets 13 right, and — the part that matters more — it never rewrote a question that was already complete.

Cost and speed

cost per question — $0.000517

Measured from the actual token counts, not estimated: about 3,000 words of chunks in, a short answer out.

It is why a 50-cent daily budget is roughly a thousand questions, and why this demo can be public without being expensive.

time to first word — 657 ms

How long before text starts appearing, against 1.76 s for the whole answer.

Measured from when the request arrives, not from when the model starts — the reader waits through the search too, and timing from the convenient moment would flatter the number by exactly the part streaming does not help.

Full methodology, including the measurements that produced negative results, is in the repository’s docs/results.md.
